2 · Data

What?

Tick the layers you want on the sheet. The first five come from open sources and are fetched for you; the last three are yours, as a spreadsheet saved as CSV.

Every layer is one of four shapes: a field (values on a grid, like rain), marks (values on places, like a region's harvest), events (things with a date, like a law), or a series (one figure per year, like exports).

Any Eurostat table as a layer deaths by week, population, unemployment, nights spent, water abstraction — type the dataset code from the Eurostat browser and it is checked here, live.

iddatasetfiltersmonthsfold

Instruments — the dated layer laws, plans, decrees, dams, programmes. A date, a title and a citation are required; a person marks confidence later.

Have your own data? Most research does. Save a sheet as CSV with the columns shown on the cards above — a place, a year and a value is enough for marks; a date, a title and a citation is enough for events. Anything you cannot map to those shapes is a good question to ask on GitHub.
3 · Years

When?

One axis for every layer. "Normal" is what a year is compared against — the WMO standard is 1991–2020. A water year runs October to September in most of the Mediterranean; pick January for a plain calendar year.

Which years, and what counts as normal?

4 · Take it

Your files

Three small files describe your study. One command on a laptop with Node fetches the data, draws the ground and the rivers, and checks every figure. The result is a page like the Iberia example — yours to host anywhere.

Submitand Layercake builds it: your files become a pull request, a person merges, the readers run, the study appears on the globe — public, cited, yours
Or downloadthe three files and run one command on your own laptop
Then openthe study page, print the plate, cite the folder

Submissions are public from the moment they are merged: the study folder lives in the open repository. Keep private data out; put it in as your own CSV on your own machine instead.
